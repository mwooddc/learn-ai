Stop 3 · A single neuron

How can numbers make a decision?

You now know that everything a computer handles is numbers. So what's the simplest thing you could do with numbers that looks like deciding? The answer is only two steps long, and it's the building block of every modern AI.

~15 minutesMultiplying and adding only

Part 1 · Weigh it up

Should you go to the park?

It's after school and you're deciding whether to go to the park. Two completely different kinds of number go into this decision.

1. Today's facts the inputs

Is it sunny? Are your friends going? Is homework due? You don't choose these. The day does. Each is a yes-or-no question, so the computer stores yes as 1 and no as 0.

Flipping them below is like trying out a different afternoon.

2. How much each fact matters to you the weights

This is about you, not the day. Some people love sunshine; some don't care.

+5 a huge reason to go · 0 doesn't matter to me · −5 a huge reason to stay

Try these. Each one ticks when you've done it.

  1. Change a fact from Yes to No. What happens to its part of the sum?

  2. Set one weight to 0. Now flip that fact on and off. Does it make any difference?

  3. Make the sum negative.

That's step one of a neuron: multiply each input by its weight, then add them all up. A "yes" (1) lets its weight count in full. A "no" (0) cancels its weight out, because anything × 0 = 0.

Here the inputs are only ever 0 or 1, because they're yes/no questions. Inputs can be other numbers too, like a temperature, or the brightness of a pixel. You'll see that in Part 5.

Part 2 · Make the call

Add a threshold

A score isn't a decision yet. So we add step two: a threshold. If the score reaches the threshold, the answer is GO. If it doesn't, the answer is STAY. We've carried over the weights you chose in Part 1.

What you've just built is called an artificial neuron. That's the whole thing: multiply, add, compare. It takes numbers in, weighs them up, and makes a yes-or-no decision.

Inputs the numbers coming in
Weights how much each input matters
Sum multiply each input by its weight and add up
Threshold is the sum big enough to say yes?
Why is it called a neuron?Go deeper

Your brain is made of around 86 billion cells called neurons. Each one receives signals from many others. Some signals excite it and some calm it down. If the total excitement is strong enough, it "fires" and sends a signal of its own.

In 1943, two scientists, Warren McCulloch and Walter Pitts, described a very simplified maths version of this, much like the one you just built. In 1958, Frank Rosenblatt built a machine called the Perceptron that used artificial neurons to recognise simple shapes. Real brain cells are far more complicated, but the name stuck.

Look at what you just did. You never saw inside Sam's head. You looked at examples, spotted where the neuron was wrong, and nudged the numbers until it was right every time. That is exactly what "learning" means for a machine. Hold that thought.

The threshold in disguiseGo deeper

Engineers usually don't use a threshold. Instead they subtract it from the sum up front and call it a bias. Then the rule is always "fire if the total is 0 or more". "Sum ≥ 3" and "sum − 3 ≥ 0" mean exactly the same thing, but this way the bias is just one more number to adjust, like a weight.

Part 5 · A neuron that looks

25 inputs, 25 weights

A neuron doesn't care what its inputs mean. They could be yes/no questions, or they could be pixels. Here's a neuron looking at a tiny 5 × 5 picture: 25 inputs, each with its own weight. Its job is to answer one question: is there a line down the middle?

Try this: draw a line across instead. Draw a line down the middle but make it wonky. Draw a very thick line. The neuron is fussy. It only fires for pictures that closely match its weights. (Much later we'll see that teams of neurons can be far less fussy.)

Choosing 25 weights by hand was just about possible, because we knew what a line looks like. But the 16 × 16 cat from Stop 1 would need 256 weights, and a phone photo would need millions. And what weights would even mean "cat"? Nobody could set those by hand.

So here's the big question for the next stop: could a machine find its own weights, the way you did for Sam, by learning from its mistakes?

What you now know

  1. An artificial neuron multiplies each input by a weight, adds them up, and compares the total with a threshold.
  2. The weights are the neuron's knowledge. Same inputs, different weights, different decision.
  3. Inputs can be anything turned into numbers, including pixels.
  4. You found Sam's weights by trying, checking against examples, and nudging. Machines need a way to do that for themselves.
