Stop 1 · Rules break

Why can't we just program a computer to be smart?

Computers follow instructions perfectly. So to build an intelligent machine, surely we just write down the right instructions? You're going to try that and find out exactly where it goes wrong.

~12 minutesNo maths needed

Part 1 · You already know how

Spot the scams

You're a moderator for an online game chat. Some messages are from friends. Some are scams trying to steal accounts. Sort them. Go as fast as you like.

    Part 2 · Write the rules

    Now teach a computer to do it

    You sorted those without thinking. But a computer can't "just know" anything. It can only follow exact instructions, and it follows them very fast, millions of times a second, without getting tired.

    One instruction a computer can follow perfectly is: does this message contain these letters? So let's build a scam filter out of rules like that. You get two kinds of rule:

    Block the message if it contains a word.
    Allow the message if it contains a word, even if a Block rule matches it too. For example, with Block "free" and Allow "free on", the message "are you free on Saturday?" gets through.

    A rule looks for those exact letters anywhere in the message, ignoring capitals. So "claim" also catches "claimed", and "gem" catches "GEMS".

    Your goal: stop all the scams, and block none of the friends' messages.

      Part 3 · What just happened?

      Your rules were always one day behind

      Here's how your filter did on the first try each morning, before you'd seen that day's messages, compared with after you fixed it.

      A mistake is either a scam that got through or a friend's message that got blocked.

      Every rule you wrote came from looking at yesterday's messages. But the scammers only need to find one gap, and they change their words the moment a filter catches them. Meanwhile, ordinary messages keep tripping your rules by accident.

      1

      Rules only know what you thought of

      "FR33 G3MS" isn't "free gems", as far as your rule is concerned. A rule can't cope with anything its writer didn't imagine.

      2

      Rules fight each other

      Block "free" and you block "are you free on Saturday?". Add an allow rule to fix that and you open a gap somewhere else. and check your newest rules still work there.

      3

      The list never ends

      You wrote a handful of rules for three days and a few dozen messages. A real game might see millions of messages a day, in dozens of languages.

      This really happened: the war on spam emailGo deeper

      Early email spam filters worked exactly like yours: lists of banned words written by people. Spammers answered with misspellings, extra spaces and even pictures of words instead of text.

      In 2002 a programmer called Paul Graham suggested a different approach. Instead of people writing the rules, the computer would count how often each word appeared in spam and in normal email, and work out the rules itself from thousands of examples. Filters built this way were much harder to fool, and they kept learning as spam changed.

      Part 4 · Pictures

      Write a rule for "cat"

      A three-year-old can spot a cat. Surely a rule for it is easy? Choose the features your rule needs. The computer will say cat only if an animal has every feature you choose.

      It's a cat if it has…

      Each card lists the features you can see in that picture. A crossed-out feature can't be seen. With nothing chosen, your rule calls every animal a cat.

      No. Look at the walking cat, the fox and the husky. They tick exactly the same boxes, so any rule that says "cat" to one says "cat" to all three. And every feature you add to shut out the dogs also shuts out a real cat: the sleeping one, the hairless one, the one facing away.

      The things that actually make a cat look like a cat (the shape of the face, the way it moves) are almost impossible to put into words, even though your brain recognises them instantly.

      Part 5 · Who ticks the boxes?

      The computer has never seen an ear

      In that last game we cheated. We told the computer which animals had pointy ears. In real life nobody does that. A camera doesn't hand the computer "whiskers: yes". It hands over this:

      A tiny 16 × 16 picture of a cat's face.

      That's it. 256 numbers, each from 0 (black) to 255 (white). There's no "ear" in there, just numbers next to other numbers. Try writing a rule for "pointy ear" using only those numbers. And a real photo has millions of them.

      So in the end, people gave up trying to write the rules. They asked a different question: what if the computer could find the rules itself, just by looking at lots of examples? That idea is called machine learning, and it's what the rest of this journey is about.

      The age of "expert systems"Go deeper

      In the 1970s and 80s, much of AI research meant exactly what you just did: sitting down with human experts and turning their knowledge into long lists of IF… THEN… rules. These expert systems were used for jobs like suggesting treatments for blood infections or deciding how to put together big computer orders. One of them grew to thousands of rules.

      They worked well in narrow areas, but they were expensive to keep up to date, and they failed in surprising ways whenever a situation came up that nobody had written a rule for. Sound familiar?

      What you now know

      1. Computers follow instructions perfectly, but only the instructions someone thought to write.
      2. The real world is too messy for hand-written rules. Words change, animals curl up, and rules clash with each other.
      3. To a computer, a picture (or a sound, or a word) is just numbers. Any intelligence has to be built out of those numbers.
