Stop 4 · Learning from mistakes

Can a machine find its own weights?

In Stop 3 you copied Sam's decisions by nudging weights until the neuron matched every afternoon. You did the learning. Now you'll turn what you did into a rule so simple that a computer can follow it, and watch the computer learn by itself.

~15 minutesBuilds on Stop 3

Part 1 · Which way to nudge?

Fix one mistake

Here's a brand-new neuron for Sam. It knows nothing yet: every weight is 0 and the threshold is 1. So for every afternoon, the sum is 0, which is below 1, and it says STAY.

On this afternoon it was sunny and Sam went to the park. The neuron got it wrong. Use the +1 and −1 buttons to make one change that fixes it.

Now the opposite mistake. A different neuron says GO on an afternoon when it was sunny but homework was due, and Sam stayed at home. Fix it with one change.

You've just discovered the two things that matter when fixing a mistake:

Which way? If the neuron was too cautious (said STAY but Sam went), the sum needs to go up or the threshold down. If it was too eager (said GO but Sam stayed), the opposite.

Which weights? Only the inputs that were yes (1) that afternoon. A "no" input is × 0, so changing its weight can't change this answer.

Part 2 · The learning rule

A recipe a computer can follow

A computer can't "have a think" about what to change. It needs exact steps. Here they are, using what you just discovered. First, two words we'll use:

  • Example: one afternoon where we know what Sam actually did.
  • Round: going through all the examples once.
  1. Show the neuron one example and let it make its decision.
  2. If it's right, change nothing. Move on to the next example.
  3. If it's wrong, nudge:
    The mistakeWeights of every yes inputThreshold
    Too cautious
    said STAY, Sam went    		+1 each−1 (easier to say GO)
    Too eager
    said GO, Sam stayed    		−1 each+1 (harder to say GO)
  4. Repeat for every example, round after round, until a whole round has no mistakes.

That's it. Notice that each nudge is small (just 1) and it only fixes things a little. The neuron gets better by making lots of small corrections, not one big jump.

Who invented this?Go deeper

This is almost exactly the rule Frank Rosenblatt used for his Perceptron in 1958, the machine you met in Stop 3. It was one of the first times a machine learned from examples rather than being given its rules. Newspapers at the time got very excited, perhaps a little too excited, about "thinking machines".

Part 4 · Watch it learn to see

Teach it X from O

In Stop 3 you looked at a neuron with 25 inputs, one for each pixel of a 5 × 5 picture, and we chose its weights. This time, nobody chooses them. All 25 weights start at 0, and the neuron learns from these 16 examples using exactly the same recipe. Its job: say X (fire) for an X and O (don't fire) for an O.

Press Step to show the neuron the first example.

Watch the weights: green means "ink here makes me think X", red means "ink here makes me think O". Nobody told the neuron what an X looks like. The pattern appears from the nudges alone.

Now test it. Draw anything and see what it thinks.

It has never seen your drawing. It can only use the weights it learned from those 16 examples. Can you draw something that fools it?

Part 5 · What this can't do

Jordan's tricky rule

Jordan goes to the park if it's sunny or friends are going, but not when both are true, because then the park is too crowded. Four examples, two inputs. Surely easy?

Why can't any weights work?Go deeper

"Sunny only" must reach the threshold, and so must "friends only". "Neither" (sum 0) must not, so the threshold must be bigger than 0. But then "both" adds the sunny weight and the friends weight together. That's at least the threshold plus the threshold, which is definitely enough to say GO. Yet Jordan stays. One neuron can never get all four right, however long it learns.

Two limits to remember. First, one neuron can't learn every pattern. Later, in Stop 6, you'll see how teams of neurons solve this.

Second, look at the recipe again. It only knows right or wrong. A near miss gets exactly the same nudge as a terrible miss. What if a machine could tell how wrong it was, and adjust by just the right amount? That's the next stop.

What you now know

  1. Learning means adjusting weights after mistakes, a small nudge in the direction that would have helped.
  2. Only the inputs that were yes get nudged, because only they affected the answer.
  3. The same simple recipe works for 3 weights or 25, and in principle for millions.
  4. The machine found weights nobody gave it, just from examples. That's machine learning.
  5. But one neuron has limits, and "right or wrong" is a blunt way to learn.
